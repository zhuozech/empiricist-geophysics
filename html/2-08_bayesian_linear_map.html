2.08 · Bayesian linear inversion and the MAP¶

2.07 ended with a problem least squares cannot solve on its own: more slip patterns than the data can see. The Bayesian answer adds what we know before the data, a prior, and returns a whole distribution of slip models instead of one. For a linear forward model with Gaussian prior and noise this distribution is Gaussian, has a closed form, and its peak (the MAP, maximum a posteriori) is exactly a regularised least-squares solution: least squares plus a penalty that discourages implausible models (2.09). This notebook builds that result from one unknown up to the 30-patch fault.

Words this notebook defines: prior, precision, posterior, precision-weighted average, MAP (maximum a posteriori), objective function, model-space and data-space forms, Kalman gain, posterior covariance, mode, median, mean, loss function, Laplace approximation, information gain per parameter.

Before this: 2.04, 2.07 (and 2.06 for the matrix steps). · You will be able to: combine a Gaussian prior and a linear forward model into the closed-form posterior; write the MAP as regularised least squares; choose between the model-space and data-space forms; say which summary (mean, median, MAP) answers which question; judge when a Laplace approximation is safe. · Optional sections: the timing comparison in section 4.

1. One unknown, one measurement, worked by hand¶

In plain words. Before measuring you think the slip on a patch is about 1 m, give or take 1 m. A measurement says 2.4 m, give or take 0.6 m. The combined answer lies between the two, closer to the more certain one, and it is more certain than either. Certainty adds up: each source contributes its precision, one over its variance, and the precisions simply add.

Precisely. Prior $m \sim N(m_0, \sigma_m^2)$, datum $d = m + e$ with $e \sim N(0, \sigma_d^2)$. The posterior is Gaussian with

$$\frac{1}{\tilde\sigma^2} = \frac{1}{\sigma_m^2} + \frac{1}{\sigma_d^2}, \qquad \bar m = \tilde\sigma^2\left(\frac{m_0}{\sigma_m^2} + \frac{d}{\sigma_d^2}\right) = w\,m_0 + (1 - w)\,d, \quad w = \frac{\tilde\sigma^2}{\sigma_m^2}.$$

Worked example (inputs: $m_0$ = 1 m, $\sigma_m$ = 1 m, $d$ = 2.4 m, $\sigma_d$ = 0.6 m):

How to read it. Left: the posterior peak lies between the prior mean and the measurement, closer to the measurement because it is more precise; it is narrower than both curves. Right: the posterior precision is the sum of the two precisions, which is why the posterior can never be less certain than its most certain ingredient.

Common misreadings.

  • "The posterior standard deviation is an average of the two." Precisions add; standard deviations do not average.
  • "The posterior mean is halfway between prior and data." It is a weighted average, with weights set by precision.

In geodesy. Every linear-Gaussian slip inversion does this sum for thousands of patch combinations at once; the matrix formulas below are this section with vectors.

2. Two unknowns: prior × likelihood = posterior¶

In plain words. Two neighbouring slip patches seen by two stations that sit far away and see both patches almost equally (the trade-off problem of notebook 2.02). The prior is a round blob; the likelihood alone is a long thin ellipse, because the data fix the sum of the two slips well and their difference poorly. Their product, the posterior, takes the best of each: narrow across from the data, limited along by the prior.

Precisely. For $\mathbf d = \mathbf G\mathbf m + \mathbf e$, $\mathbf e \sim N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf C_d)$, $\mathbf m \sim N(\mathbf m_0, \mathbf C_m)$:

$$p(\mathbf m \mid \mathbf d) \propto \underbrace{\exp\!\big[-\tfrac12(\mathbf d-\mathbf G\mathbf m)^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}(\mathbf d-\mathbf G\mathbf m)\big]}_{\text{likelihood}} \;\underbrace{\exp\!\big[-\tfrac12(\mathbf m-\mathbf m_0)^\top\mathbf C_m^{-1}(\mathbf m-\mathbf m_0)\big]}_{\text{prior}},$$

a Gaussian $N(\bar{\mathbf m}, \tilde{\mathbf C}_m)$ with precision $\tilde{\mathbf C}_m^{-1} = \mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf G + \mathbf C_m^{-1}$: precisions add, as in section 1.

3. The MAP: the lowest point of misfit plus prior penalty¶

In plain words. Take minus the logarithm of the posterior. It becomes a sum of two penalties: how badly the model misfits the data, and how far the model strays from the prior. The single most probable model, the MAP, is the model with the smallest total penalty. That is a regularised least-squares problem, so a regularised inversion is a Bayesian MAP with a particular prior (2.09 lists which regulariser corresponds to which prior).

Precisely.

$$-\ln p(\mathbf m \mid \mathbf d) = \underbrace{\tfrac12(\mathbf d-\mathbf G\mathbf m)^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}(\mathbf d-\mathbf G\mathbf m)}_{\Phi_d:\ \text{misfit}} + \underbrace{\tfrac12(\mathbf m-\mathbf m_0)^\top\mathbf C_m^{-1}(\mathbf m-\mathbf m_0)}_{\Phi_m:\ \text{prior penalty}} + \text{const},$$

(arg min: the $\mathbf m$ that makes the sum smallest; setting its gradient to zero gives the linear system on the right)

$$\hat{\mathbf m} = \arg\min(\Phi_d + \Phi_m) \;\Longleftrightarrow\; (\mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf G + \mathbf C_m^{-1})\,\hat{\mathbf m} = \mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf d + \mathbf C_m^{-1}\mathbf m_0 .$$

For a linear-Gaussian problem the MAP equals the posterior mean and the median. To make the picture clear we use the flat-direction problem of notebook 2.02: one datum that sees only $m_1 + m_2$, so least squares alone has a whole line of equally good answers (a null space, 2.07), and the prior has to choose.

Worked example. Datum $d$ = 1 (σ = 0.15) of $m_1 + m_2$; prior mean (0.8, −0.4), prior sd 1 for each patch.

How to read it. (a) The misfit has a valley whose floor is the whole orange line: every point on it fits the datum perfectly, so least squares cannot choose. (b) The prior penalty is a bowl around the prior mean. (c) Their sum has a single lowest point, the MAP. As the prior weight shrinks, the MAP slides along the blue path to the point of the least-squares line closest to the prior mean (printed above); as it grows, the MAP moves to the prior mean. With weak priors this limit is the minimum-norm least-squares answer of 2.07 when the prior mean is zero: that "neutral" answer was a prior all along.

Common misreadings.

  • "Regularisation is a numerical trick, separate from Bayes." Minimising misfit + penalty is finding the MAP of a posterior whose prior is $\exp(-\text{penalty})$.
  • "The MAP is the answer." It is one point. The posterior also says how far other models may be from it, which the MAP alone hides (section 5 shows a case where the MAP is not even typical).

4. Two equal formulas, and when to use which¶

In plain words. The same posterior can be computed in two ways. One works in the space of unknowns and inverts a matrix as large as the number of unknowns; the other works in the space of data and inverts a matrix as large as the number of data. The answers are identical; the cost is not. Use the smaller space.

Precisely. Model-space form: $$\tilde{\mathbf C}_m = (\mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf G + \mathbf C_m^{-1})^{-1},\qquad \bar{\mathbf m} = \tilde{\mathbf C}_m(\mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf d + \mathbf C_m^{-1}\mathbf m_0).$$ Data-space form, with the Kalman gain $\mathbf K = \mathbf C_m\mathbf G^\top(\mathbf G\mathbf C_m\mathbf G^\top + \mathbf C_d)^{-1}$: $$\bar{\mathbf m} = \mathbf m_0 + \mathbf K(\mathbf d - \mathbf G\mathbf m_0),\qquad \tilde{\mathbf C}_m = \mathbf C_m - \mathbf K\mathbf G\mathbf C_m .$$ The two are equal by the Woodbury matrix identity (an algebraic rule that rewrites the inverse of a sum of matrices). The first inverts an $N_m \times N_m$ matrix, the second an $N_d \times N_d$ one. (2.04 shows the gain arising as the conditional mean of a joint Gaussian; 2.23 uses it in time.)

    return (np.arctan(z2 / x) - np.arctan(z1 / x)) / np.pi


Gf = 1000.0 * np.array([[patch_disp(x, z_edges[j], z_edges[j + 1]) for j in range(Nf)] for x in xs])
s_true = 2.0 * np.exp(-0.5 * ((zc - 12.0) / 4.0) ** 2)
sig_f = 3.0

def model_space(G, d, cd, m0, Cm):
    """Posterior mean and covariance in model space; cd = diagonal of C_d (independent noise)."""
    Cpost = np.linalg.inv(G.T @ (G / cd[:, None]) + np.linalg.inv(Cm))   # invert an N_m x N_m matrix
    return Cpost @ (G.T @ (d / cd) + np.linalg.solve(Cm, m0)), Cpost


def data_space(G, d, cd, m0, Cm):
    """The same in data space with the Kalman gain; cd = diagonal of C_d."""
    S_ = G @ Cm @ G.T + np.diag(cd)                                     # an N_d x N_d matrix
    K = np.linalg.solve(S_, G @ Cm).T                                   # K = Cm G^T S^-1
    return m0 + K @ (d - G @ m0), Cm - K @ G @ Cm


Cd_f = np.full(xs.size, sig_f ** 2)
m0_f, Cm_f = np.zeros(Nf), np.eye(Nf)                      # iid prior, sd 1 m
ma, Ca = model_space(Gf, d_f, Cd_f, m0_f, Cm_f)

def timed(f, *args, reps=3):
    best = np.inf
    for _ in range(reps):
        t0 = time.perf_counter(); f(*args); best = min(best, time.perf_counter() - t0)
    return best

How to read it. With many unknowns and few data, the data-space form is the cheap one; with few unknowns and many data, the model-space form is. Both give the same mean to round-off (printed). Times are for this machine and change with the hardware, but the ranking follows the matrix sizes.

Common misreadings.

  • "The Kalman-gain formula is only for filters." It is the static Bayesian update written in data space; filters (2.23) apply it repeatedly.
  • "Both forms are equally stable." With a nearly flat prior, $\mathbf C_m - \mathbf K\mathbf G\mathbf C_m$ can lose precision (notebook 2.33 meets this); the precision (model-space) form or the Joseph form (a rearrangement of the same update that stays symmetric and positive definite under round-off) is safer there.

In practice. The ETKF (ensemble transform Kalman filter) form of ensemble Kalman inversion does a data-space-style update in the space of ensemble members (a $J \times J$ matrix), which is why it stays affordable with thousands of unknowns (2.24).

5. MAP, mean and median are different when the posterior is not Gaussian¶

In plain words. Slip on a thrust (a fault where one block is pushed up over the other) cannot be negative. Suppose the prior says "positive, usually below a couple of metres" and one noisy measurement comes out slightly negative. The single most probable value is then exactly zero slip, right at the boundary. But the average of all plausible slips is clearly positive, because every plausible value is zero or more. Which number you report depends on the question you ask.

Precisely. The mean minimises the expected squared error $\mathbb E(m - \hat m)^2$, and the median the expected absolute error $\mathbb E|m - \hat m|$. The MAP is often said to minimise the expected "0-1 loss" (1 for a miss, 0 for a hit), but that is exact only for discrete parameters. For a continuous posterior every single value has probability zero, so under an exact 0-1 loss every estimate scores the same. The MAP is instead the limit of the best estimate under a loss that counts a hit within a tolerance ε, as ε shrinks to zero (for well-behaved densities). It also has a property the mean lacks: it moves when the parameter is rewritten in other units, for example slip versus log slip (2.11 and 2.32 show this). For a symmetric single-peaked posterior (any Gaussian) the three summaries coincide; for a skewed or bounded one they do not.

Worked example (inputs: prior HalfNormal with scale 1 m, a Gaussian folded at zero so that only non-negative values remain (2.11); datum $d$ = −0.2 m, σ = 0.5 m). The posterior is a Gaussian truncated at zero; we compute it on a fine grid and compare with the truncated-normal formulas.

6. The Laplace approximation: a Gaussian fitted at the MAP¶

In plain words. For a nonlinear problem there is no closed form, but one can still find the MAP and measure how sharply the posterior curves around it. Replacing the log posterior by a parabola with that curvature gives a Gaussian approximation, the Laplace approximation. It is exact when the problem is linear and misleading when the posterior bends.

Precisely. With $\hat{\mathbf m}$ the MAP and $\mathbf H = -\nabla^2 \ln p(\mathbf m\mid\mathbf d)\big|_{\hat{\mathbf m}}$ the Hessian (the matrix of second derivatives: the curvature of the log posterior in every pair of directions), $p(\mathbf m\mid\mathbf d) \approx N(\hat{\mathbf m}, \mathbf H^{-1})$. For the linear-Gaussian problem $\mathbf H = \mathbf G^\top\mathbf C_d^{-1}\mathbf G + \mathbf C_m^{-1}$ exactly. (Notebook 2.02 met this ellipse; here we look at why it fails.)

7. How much did the data teach us, patch by patch?¶

In plain words. Compare each patch's uncertainty after the data with its uncertainty before. A ratio near zero means the data pinned that patch down; a ratio near one means the data said nothing about it, and the answer there is the prior. The same comparison can be counted in bits, the unit of information: halving the standard deviation is one bit (2.27 develops this).

Precisely. Marginal ratio $\tilde\sigma_j/\sigma_{m,j}$ and marginal information gain $\log_2(\sigma_{m,j}/\tilde\sigma_j)$ bits. The joint gain $\tfrac12\log_2(\lvert\mathbf C_m\rvert/\lvert\tilde{\mathbf C}_m\rvert)$ counts what was learned about all patches together (the determinant $\lvert\mathbf C\rvert$, 2.06, measures the volume of the uncertainty ellipsoid). With an independent prior the joint gain is never smaller than the sum of the marginal gains: the data also teach about combinations of patches that no single patch shows (in 2.27's language, entropy is subadditive). With a correlated prior the sum can exceed the joint gain, because the same information is counted again at every correlated neighbour.

In [13]:
def matern(z, ell, nu=1.0, amp=1.0):
    r = np.abs(z[:, None] - z[None, :])
    x = np.sqrt(2 * nu) * r / ell
    with np.errstate(invalid="ignore", divide="ignore"):
        C = amp ** 2 * 2 ** (1 - nu) / gamma(nu) * x ** nu * kv(nu, x)
    C[r == 0] = amp ** 2
    return C + 1e-9 * np.eye(z.size)

How to read it. Shallow patches lose most of their prior uncertainty; deep ones keep most of it, so their posterior there is mostly the prior. The correlated (Matérn) prior, one in which neighbouring patches are correlated over a set distance (2.09, 2.12), spreads what the data say about one patch to its neighbours, so individual patches look better determined. The printed totals show both directions of the precise statement above: with the iid prior (independent and identically distributed: every patch has the same prior and none is correlated) the joint gain is larger than the sum of the per-patch gains (the data fix combinations such as the sum of neighbouring slips), with the Matérn prior the per-patch sum is larger (the same information counted at each correlated neighbour). Judge what the data taught from the joint number, or from the per-patch ratio read together with the prior.

Common misreadings.

  • "A small posterior sd at depth means the data constrain deep slip." Compare it with the prior sd first. If the ratio is near 1 the number came from the prior.

In practice. Showing prior and posterior side by side (as notebook 2.33, section 8, does with the resolution matrix) is the quickest way to tell readers which features of a slip model come from the data.
