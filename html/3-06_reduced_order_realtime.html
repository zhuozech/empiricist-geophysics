3.06 · Reduced-order models and real-time inference¶

Topic 3 is under development: content and numbering may still change.

Before this: 2.06 (SVD), 2.08 (Bayesian linear inversion and the gain form), 3.01 (PCA of outputs), 3.05 (the 1-D fault model) · You will be able to: compress solutions with POD and read the energy curve; build a data-driven and a Galerkin reduced model and judge their accuracy and speed; precompute an exact linear Bayesian inversion so that each new data set costs one matrix-vector product · Optional sections: 4 (outside the training range).

3.05 replaced the fault solver with a network that knows nothing about the equations. This notebook takes the other road: keep the physics, but find out how few numbers really describe its solutions, and solve for those few. That is a reduced-order model (ROM). The second half asks what "real time" can mean for an inversion: when the forward model is linear, everything except one matrix-vector product can be done before the earthquake, and the answer is still exact.

Words this notebook defines: snapshot, proper orthogonal decomposition (POD), mode, energy, projection error, full-order model (FOM), data-driven (non-intrusive) ROM, radial basis function (RBF) interpolation, Galerkin projection (intrusive ROM), hyper-reduction, discrete empirical interpolation (DEIM), offline and online phases, quantity of interest (QoI), goal-oriented inference.

1. Snapshots and POD: how many shapes does afterslip need?¶

In plain words. Take every slip profile the solver produced (every run, every epoch) and stack them. If afterslip always looks like some mixture of a few basic shapes, a few numbers per profile are enough: the weights of those shapes. The singular value decomposition (SVD, 2.06) finds the best shapes, ordered from most to least important.

Precisely. Collect the snapshots as rows of $\mathbf S\in\mathbb R^{M\times N_f}$ ($M$ = runs × epochs) and take the SVD $\mathbf S = \mathbf U\boldsymbol\Sigma\mathbf V^\top$. The first $r$ columns of $\mathbf V$ form the POD basis $\boldsymbol\Psi_r$ (the modes). Any profile is approximated by its projection $\mathbf s \approx \boldsymbol\Psi_r\boldsymbol\Psi_r^\top\mathbf s$, and over the snapshots the squared error equals the discarded "energy" $\sum_{k>r}\sigma_k^2$. We do not subtract a mean, so the zero profile at $t = 0$ is represented exactly. POD is the same algebra as principal component analysis (3.01) applied to solution snapshots.

"Best" has a precise meaning here: the projection $\boldsymbol\Psi_r\boldsymbol\Psi_r^\top\mathbf s$ is the r-mode profile closest to $\mathbf s$ in slip (the state), summed over patches. An inversion sees the surface instead, $\mathbf G_f\mathbf s$. The r-mode profile that best reproduces the surface displacement uses different coefficients, $\mathbf b^\star = \arg\min_{\mathbf b}\lVert\mathbf G_f\boldsymbol\Psi_r\mathbf b - \mathbf G_f\mathbf s\rVert$ (a least-squares problem, 2.07), and its surface error can only be smaller. Both are computed below.

def surface_best(r):
    """The r-mode slip whose surface displacement is closest to the true one: Psi b*, b* = pinv(G Psi) G s."""
    Psi = Vt[:r].T
    M = Psi @ np.linalg.pinv(Gf @ Psi, rcond=1e-10) @ Gf       # (nf, nf): slip -> best r-mode slip for the surface

    # singular G Psi makes the error grow again with r, although the best fit over nested sets of modes cannot get worse
    return sf[TE] @ M.T

2. A data-driven ROM: interpolate the POD coefficients¶

In plain words. If every solution is a few POD coefficients, a ROM can skip the equations altogether: learn the map from the input parameters to those coefficients from the training runs, and interpolate for new inputs. This is what Magen et al. (2026) did for slow-slip cycles in Cascadia, with RBF interpolation. It never touches the solver code, which is why it is called non-intrusive.

Precisely. Each run's whole history (40 epochs × 31 patches) is one vector; POD over runs (mean removed) gives $r = 16$ coefficients per run. The input, a 40-value profile, is reduced to its leading $k_x$ principal components. A radial basis function (RBF) interpolant predicts at a new input by a weighted sum of bumps centred on the training inputs, each depending only on the distance to its centre, with weights chosen so that the training runs are reproduced exactly. Here the bump is the thin-plate spline $r^2\log r$ (scipy.interpolate.RBFInterpolator, plus a linear polynomial), and it maps the $k_x$ input numbers to the 16 coefficients. We vary $k_x$, because an interpolant has to fill a $k_x$-dimensional space with training runs.

def rbf_rom(idx_tr, kx, r=R_OUT):
    ym = Yflat[idx_tr].mean(0)
    _, _, Vy = np.linalg.svd(Yflat[idx_tr] - ym, full_matrices=False)
    Zx = (la_all - xm) @ Vx[:kx].T
    rb = RBFInterpolator(Zx[idx_tr], (Yflat[idx_tr] - ym) @ Vy[:r].T, kernel="thin_plate_spline", degree=1)
    return lambda la_: (ym + rb(np.atleast_2d((la_ - xm) @ Vx[:kx].T)) @ Vy[:r]).reshape(-1, len(T), nf)

3. An intrusive ROM: project the equations¶

In plain words. Instead of learning outputs, keep solving the friction equations, but only for the weights of the r POD shapes. The ODE for 31 slips becomes an ODE for r numbers. Because the physics is still solved, the ROM works for any friction profile; the only approximation is that slip must be a mixture of the r shapes.

Precisely. On the free patches, pinned patches do not slip, so stress is a linear function of slip, $\boldsymbol\tau_f = \Delta\boldsymbol\tau_f + \mathbf K_{ff}\,\mathbf s_f$, and the full-order model (FOM) is $\dot{\mathbf s}_f = 2v_0\sinh\big((\Delta\boldsymbol\tau_f + \mathbf K_{ff}\mathbf s_f)/a\sigma\big)$, $\mathbf s_f(0) = 0$. Galerkin projection substitutes $\mathbf s_f = \boldsymbol\Psi_r\mathbf b$ and keeps only the part of the equation that lies along the modes (multiplying it by $\boldsymbol\Psi_r^\top$), which gives $$\dot{\mathbf b} = \boldsymbol\Psi_r^\top\,2v_0\sinh\!\big((\Delta\boldsymbol\tau_f + \mathbf K_{ff}\boldsymbol\Psi_r\mathbf b)/a\sigma\big),\qquad \mathbf b(0) = \mathbf 0 .$$ The right side still evaluates $\sinh$ on all 31 patches. Hyper-reduction removes that cost: DEIM (Chaturantabut & Sorensen 2010, cited for the idea only) builds a POD basis $\mathbf U_m$ of the slip-rate snapshots, picks $m$ patches greedily, and reconstructs the whole slip-rate vector from those $m$ values, $\mathbf v \approx \mathbf U_m(\mathbf P^\top\mathbf U_m)^{-1}\mathbf P^\top\mathbf v$. The ROM then evaluates $\sinh$ at $m$ patches only. Both ROMs use the same stiff solver as the FOM (Kvaerno5, an implicit solver suited to stiff equations, 3.04; relative tolerance $10^{-5}$).

def deim_points(U):
    """Greedy DEIM selection: each new point is where the current interpolant of the next basis vector is worst."""
    p = [int(np.argmax(np.abs(U[:, 0])))]
    for j in range(1, U.shape[1]):
        c = np.linalg.solve(U[p, :j], U[p, j])
        p.append(int(np.argmax(np.abs(U[:, j] - U[:, :j] @ c))))
    return np.array(p)


def make_rom(r, m=None):
    Psi = jnp.asarray(Vt[:r].T)
    KP, d0 = jnp.asarray(Kff) @ Psi, jnp.asarray(dtf)
    if m is None:
        rhs_v = lambda b, a: Psi.T @ (2 * 0.03 * jnp.sinh((d0 + KP @ b) / a))
        sel = jnp.arange(nf)
    else:
        p = deim_points(Uv[:, :m])
        M = jnp.asarray(Vt[:r] @ Uv[:, :m] @ np.linalg.inv(Uv[p, :m]))
        KPp, d0p, sel = KP[p], d0[p], jnp.asarray(p)
        rhs_v = lambda b, a: M @ (2 * 0.03 * jnp.sinh((d0p + KPp @ b) / a))

    @jax.jit
    def solve(la_f):
        a = jnp.exp(la_f)[sel]
        sol = dfx.diffeqsolve(dfx.ODETerm(lambda t, b, args: rhs_v(b, a)), dfx.Kvaerno5(), t0=0.0, t1=T[-1], dt0=1e-8,
                              y0=jnp.zeros(r), saveat=dfx.SaveAt(ts=jnp.asarray(T)),
                              stepsize_controller=dfx.PIDController(rtol=1e-5, atol=1e-7), max_steps=400_000)
        return sol.ys @ Psi.T
    return solve


def run_timed(f, inputs):
    jax.block_until_ready(f(inputs[0]))                      # compile first, then time
    t1 = time.time()
    out = np.stack([np.asarray(f(x)) for x in inputs])
    return out, (time.time() - t1) / len(inputs)

Common misreadings.

  • "The speed-up is the ratio of unknowns." The cost of a stiff solver depends on the number of time steps as much as on the size of the system; at 31 unknowns projection buys accuracy control, not speed. It pays when the FOM has thousands to millions of unknowns (Magen et al. quote 360,000× for a data-driven ROM of a volumetric model).
  • "Reduction means POD." The largest saving in the middle panel came from rewriting the equations exactly (eliminating stress and the pinned patches). Check for exact reductions before building an approximate one.
  • "DEIM is optional." For a nonlinear term it is what makes the ROM's cost independent of the full size; without it every step still touches every patch.

5. Real-time Bayesian inference: offline once, online in microseconds¶

In plain words. For a linear forward model with a Gaussian prior and Gaussian noise, the posterior mean is a fixed matrix times the data (2.08). That matrix does not depend on the data, so it can be computed before the earthquake. When data arrive, inference is one matrix-vector product, and it is exact, not approximate. If only a few quantities matter (what a warning needs), precompute the matrix that maps data straight to those quantities: that is goal-oriented inference.

Precisely. With $\mathbf d = \mathbf G\mathbf m + \mathbf e$, $\mathbf m\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf m_0, \mathbf C_m)$, $\mathbf e\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0, \mathbf C_d)$ and quantities of interest $\mathbf q = \mathbf Q\mathbf m$, $$\bar{\mathbf q} = \mathbf Q\mathbf m_0 + \underbrace{\mathbf Q\mathbf C_m\mathbf G^\top(\mathbf G\mathbf C_m\mathbf G^\top + \mathbf C_d)^{-1}}_{\mathbf A_q\ \text{(offline)}}(\mathbf d - \mathbf G\mathbf m_0),\qquad \tilde{\mathbf C}_q = \mathbf Q\mathbf C_m\mathbf Q^\top - \mathbf A_q\mathbf G\mathbf C_m\mathbf Q^\top .$$ Both $\mathbf A_q$ and $\tilde{\mathbf C}_q$ are data-independent (offline); the online step is $\mathbf A_q\mathbf d$. Forming $\mathbf G\mathbf C_m\mathbf G^\top$ needs $\mathbf G^\top$ applied to one unit vector per datum: in a PDE model (a forward model given by a partial differential equation, such as the wave equation) each is one adjoint solve (2.22), which is why Henneking et al.'s offline phase costs one adjoint wave propagation per sensor (their time-invariance lets one solve serve every time sample of a sensor).

The toy. Kinematic afterslip: the unknowns are the slip of all 40 patches at all 40 epochs (1,600 numbers; the inversion does not know the friction law or which patches are pinned). Prior: mean zero, standard deviation 0.6 m, Matérn correlation in depth (ℓ = 8 km, ν = 1.5, 2.12) times a Gaussian correlation in log-time (scale 1 in ln t). Coverage, used below, is the share of held-out runs whose true value falls inside the stated interval (2.01, 2.32). Data: 30 stations × 40 epochs, σ = 2 mm. The truth is a run of the nonlinear friction model, so the prior is not the process that made it. Quantities of interest: surface displacement after one year at 60 points between 0.3 and 150 km (most of them between stations), and the potency (slip × depth extent, summed over the fault) at every epoch.

Common misreadings.

  • "Real time needs a surrogate." Not for a linear problem with fixed hyperparameters: the exact posterior is already a matrix-vector product. Surrogates and ROMs are needed when the forward model is nonlinear or the hyperparameters are sampled, because then the gain changes with every candidate.
  • "Offline cost is unimportant." It is paid once per network and prior, before the event, and it is the expensive part (printed). Henneking et al. (2025, p. 1) report that "The offline component requires just one adjoint wave propagation per sensor", run on 43,520 GPUs.

6. Where the field is¶

Quotes are verbatim, with page numbers.

  • Exact, real-time, goal-oriented. Henneking, Venkat & Ghattas (2026, p. 1) describe the split used in section 5: they exploit "the autonomous structure of the forward model to decompose the solution of the inverse problem into a one-time-only offline phase in which the PDE model is solved a limited number of times (equal to the number of sensors), and an online phase that maps well onto GPUs and computes the parameter inference and prediction of quantities of interest in real time, given observational data." At Cascadia scale, Henneking et al. (2025, p. 1): "given real-time data, the online component exactly solves the Bayesian inverse and forecasting problems in 0.2 seconds on a modest GPU system, a ten-billion-fold speedup."
  • ROMs for slow slip. Magen, May & Gabriel (2026, p. 1), quoted in section 2: POD plus RBF interpolation over a 2-D parameter space, used inside MCMC.
  • ROMs for Green's functions. Rekoske, May & Gabriel (2024, p. 1): "The ROM constructed for this region enables rapid computation (0.001 CPU hours) of complete, high-resolution (500 m spacing), 0.5 Hz surface velocity wavefields that are accurate for a shortest wavelength of 1.0 km for a single moment tensor source."
  • Galerkin projection and DEIM are classical model-reduction tools (e.g. Chaturantabut & Sorensen 2010), cited for the idea only.

In practice.

  • An intrusive ROM still solves the friction law, so it needs no new training when the friction field changes, only a basis that contains the slip shapes; a data-driven ROM or a network has to see similar inputs in training.
  • The offline-online split of section 5 is exact only for a linear forward model with fixed hyperparameters and a Gaussian prior. If noise scales or smoothing weights are sampled, or the prior is bounded (for example slip that must be positive), the gain changes with every draw, and the split holds only conditionally on them.
  • For rapid tsunami or slip estimates from a fixed sensor network, section 5 is the whole recipe at toy scale: a linear map, a prior, a gain computed before the event, and a goal-oriented product when data arrive.
